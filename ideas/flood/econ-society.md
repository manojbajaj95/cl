# Flood: continual learning ideas from economies and societies

Raw, unfiltered pool for the novelty reviewer. Each idea borrows a mechanism from economics, markets, evolution, culture or institutions: how societies keep knowledge alive, specialize, forget and recover. Nothing here has been pre-killed. I skimmed `reviews/idea-ledger.md` ([PR #6](https://github.com/manojbajaj95/cl/pull/6)) only to avoid exact repeats; where a near neighbour exists, it is named so the reviewer can judge the difference. Each idea also names the closest same-budget control that could tie it.

Format: **mechanism** / **why new** / **cheapest first test**.

## Markets, prices and money

**E1. Georgist parameter rent.** Every parameter block pays rent equal to its *land value*: how much current-task gradient demand presses on it (for example a running mean of squared new-task gradients). A task keeps a block only while that block's marginal contribution to the task's loss beats the rent. Uncontested capacity costs nothing to hold, and contested capacity has to keep earning its place. *Why new:* EWC/SI protect by past importance and never look at present demand. Rent makes protection a price that rises and falls with contention, so stale skills sitting on hot real estate get evicted and quiet skills live for free. *First test:* permuted/split MNIST MLP with a penalty of λ·F_old·D_new per parameter against EWC at a matched mean penalty, plotting the retention–plasticity frontier over 20 tasks. Control: EWC with λ tuned per task.

**E2. Index-linked memories (a logit CPI).** Continual fine-tuning drifts the model's "price level": logit scale, margin distribution, layer-norm gains. Contracts written in old nominal units (calibrated thresholds, lesson margins, reward-model cut-offs, router biases) silently lose value. The idea is to keep a fixed basket of probe prompts, measure a per-layer price index after every update, and store every memory artefact in real (index-deflated) units, re-inflating it at read time. *Why new:* forgetting gets reframed as partly *monetary* (unit drift) rather than *real* (lost knowledge). That gives a cheap fix and a testable decomposition, and it fits A3's finding that lessons act like temperature. *First test:* sequential fine-tunes of a 0.5B model. For each old task, split its accuracy drop into the part that rescaling by the basket index (one scalar temperature per layer, fit only on the basket) restores and the remainder. Kill if the share is under 10%. Control: a per-task temperature refit on old-task data (the oracle upper bound) and a global scalar temperature.

**E3. Fractional-reserve replay with a lender of last resort.** The replay buffer works like a bank. It holds only a fraction r of old "deposits" as real examples, enough for normal withdrawal demand (old tasks reappearing at their usual rate). When a *run* is detected (many old tasks queried at once, seen as a jump in probe-loss variance), a frozen snapshot acts as the central bank and lends liquidity through a short burst of distillation. *Why new:* replay is usually a constant tax. This version is a two-tier system with a crisis regime whose trigger and size come from bank-run theory (Diamond–Dybvig). *First test:* a stream where all old tasks return together every 30 tasks. Fixed replay against fractional reserve plus snapshot bursts at equal total replay compute, measuring the worst-case dip during a run. Control: fixed replay at the same mean budget.

**E4. Emphasis hyperinflation in agent memory.** Agents that write their own lessons drift toward ever more emphatic language ("IMPORTANT", "ALWAYS", "NEVER EVER") to make new lessons win attention. That debases older lessons written in calm language, the way printing money debases savings. The primitive is a conserved *emphasis supply*: a fixed budget of salience tokens or attention-bias mass spread across all lessons, so emphasizing one lesson takes emphasis from others. *Why new:* it names and measures a monetary failure mode of self-written memory and fixes it with a conservation law instead of a pruning rule. *First test:* a Qwen2.5-1.5B agent rewriting its lesson file over 50 cycles. Count emphasis markers per cycle and measure the effect of cycle-1 lessons over time. Rerun with an emphasis budget enforced at write time. Control: same-length rewrite with emphasis words stripped, plus the A3 temperature-matched arm.

**E5. Gresham's law for retrieval.** Bad memories drive out good ones. When generic, short, low-value lessons are cheap to match (high similarity to many queries), they circulate. Specific, high-value lessons get hoarded: stored but never retrieved. A fixed "exchange rate" (cosine similarity as legal tender) causes this. A floating exchange rate, where each lesson's retrieval weight is repriced by its realized downstream value, removes it. *Why new:* it predicts a specific time course: the usage share of generic lessons rises monotonically under similarity retrieval, while specific lessons keep their value per use but lose volume. That gives a diagnostic for memory-store decay, not only a ranking heuristic. *First test:* a memory bank seeded with paired generic and specific lessons on a constrained router task. Track usage share over 1,000 episodes under cosine retrieval against value-repriced retrieval. Control: recency-weighted retrieval.

**E6. Vickrey auctions for mixture-of-experts capacity.** Experts bid for incoming examples with their predicted marginal loss reduction, under a per-task budget. Second-price clearing stops an expert that is greedy for new data from overbidding. Clearing prices persist as a memory of past specialization: an expert that has been "rich" in a domain keeps a price advantage there. *Why new:* MoE CL work uses learned gates or top-k. A budgeted truthful auction gives each old specialist a guaranteed floor that new tasks cannot outbid. *First test:* 4-expert MoE on split CIFAR-10, auction routing against top-1 gating at equal parameters, measuring old-task accuracy and the expert-collapse rate. Control: top-1 gating with load-balancing loss.

**E7. Futures market on forgetting with skin in the game.** Several small heads (traders) bet, through a logarithmic market scoring rule, on how much each old task will lose after the next update. Replay is allocated by market price. Traders' payouts become their own weight updates: correct bettors get more say in the next allocation. *Why new:* forgetting forecasts exist (ledger #16). An incentive-compatible aggregation whose participants are *parts of the model being forecast* is new, and it adds a feedback loop: replay changes outcomes, which moves prices. *First test:* 5 forecaster heads on a 20-task split stream, comparing market-price replay with mean-forecast replay and uniform replay at equal budget. Control: mean forecast, which aggregates without incentives.

**E8. Taylor-rule plasticity.** A central-bank rule sets the learning rate: LR_t = LR* + a·(output gap) − b·(inflation). The output gap is current-task loss above target. Inflation is the drift rate of outputs on a fixed probe basket. *Why new:* PID/edit-strength gating exists (ledger notes 2610.16129). The Taylor principle (b > 1, responding more than one-for-one to inflation) makes a sharp, falsifiable stability prediction. Rules that violate it should show runaway drift spirals and rules that satisfy it should not. *First test:* sweep (a, b) on a permuted-MNIST stream and check whether the b = 1 boundary separates stable from unstable retention. Control: a cosine schedule with matched mean LR.

**E9. Plasticity credit lines.** A model can *borrow* plasticity to absorb something urgent: unfreeze protected weights or take a large LR. The loan creates an explicit debt, the old-function deviation on a probe basket, which must be repaid by scheduled consolidation within T steps at an interest rate (the deviation tolerance tightens over time). Failing to repay triggers an automatic rollback (default). *Why new:* it turns the stability–plasticity tradeoff into an intertemporal contract. Urgent learning is allowed, but only with a repayment schedule, so you can learn now and consolidate later with a guarantee. *First test:* an urgent new task followed by repayment distillation against a steady low LR with the same total steps, measuring both time-to-competence and final retention. Control: steady LR, and high LR with no repayment.

**E10. Insurance pools with actuarial premiums.** Each task pays a premium (a share of the replay budget) into a pool, priced by its estimated forgetting hazard (volatility of past drops). When a task suffers a forgetting shock, the pool pays out a claim, a concentrated restoration burst. Low-risk tasks subsidize high-risk ones only up to the actuarially fair level. *Why new:* replay allocation becomes risk pooling with explicit pricing. Premiums move as hazards are revealed, which yields a calibration test: if premiums are fair, the pool breaks even. *First test:* a 20-task stream comparing pool allocation with uniform and loss-proportional replay, measuring worst-task retention. Control: loss-proportional replay.

## Firms, contracts and institutions

**E11. The Coasean boundary of the model.** When should knowledge live inside the weights (the firm) and when should it be bought from the market (retrieval, a tool, another agent)? Coase's answer: internalize when the transaction cost of fetching it each time (latency, retrieval error, context tokens × query frequency) exceeds the cost of owning it (training compute plus the interference and forgetting it causes). The primitive is a per-fact boundary rule estimated online, with facts moving in and out of the weights as frequencies change. *Why new:* RAG-vs-fine-tune is usually decided once, by hand. This is a continual, priced make-or-buy decision per item, with predictions about where the boundary sits. *First test:* a Zipf-distributed QA stream where the Coase rule decides weights vs retrieval per fact, compared with all-retrieval and all-weights at fixed compute plus context budget. Control: a frequency-threshold rule tuned on held-out data.

**E12. Bankruptcy reorganization with absolute priority.** After an edit stream collapses a model (MEMIT past capacity, many LoRA merges), declare bankruptcy. A fresh copy is re-distilled, and creditors (tasks or edits) are paid under the absolute priority rule: senior claims (safety, core skills, oldest commitments) are fully restored before junior claims get any capacity. Juniors receive partial "equity" (lower-fidelity distillation). *Why new:* recovery after collapse is usually uniform re-training. Here priority-ordered restructuring makes the trade-off explicit and predicts a sharp seniority step in post-reorganization retention. *First test:* GPT-2 with 2k MEMIT edits (collapsed), re-distilled into a fresh copy with priority ordering against a uniform mix. Measure retention by seniority class. Control: uniform re-distillation at equal steps.

**E13. Patent terms for circuits.** A newly learned skill gets exclusive, frozen protection of its top-Fisher parameters for a fixed term of T tasks, after which it enters the *public domain*. Its parameters are unfrozen, but before release the skill is distilled into a shared, compressed commons module that later skills can build on. *Why new:* permanent protection (EWC, PackNet) leads to capacity exhaustion, and no protection leads to forgetting. Fixed-term exclusivity followed by mandatory publication into a commons is a third regime, with an optimal-term prediction borrowed from Nordhaus's patent-life model. *First test:* split CIFAR-100 over 20 tasks, sweeping T, against PackNet and EWC at matched capacity. Look for an interior optimum in T. Control: EWC with a λ that decays at the same rate.

**E14. Ostrom commons governance for shared weights.** Shared parameters are a commons that every task overgrazes. Ostrom's design principles become a training rule. Each task's gradient is *monitored* for its measured harm to others over a sliding window. Repeat offenders face *graduated sanctions* (scaled LR, then projection, then exclusion from shared blocks). First offences are forgiven. *Why new:* GEM/PCGrad project every conflict at once and treat all tasks the same. Graduated sanctions keep a per-task history, punish persistent overgrazers harder, and forgive occasional conflict, which should preserve more plasticity. *First test:* a 10-task stream with one deliberately conflicting task family, sanctions against A-GEM at equal compute, measuring both forgetting and new-task learning speed. Control: A-GEM, and a constant LR scale on the conflicting family.

**E15. VCG gradient taxes.** Each task's update pays a Vickrey–Clarke–Groves tax equal to the externality it imposes on every other task: the loss increase on their probe sets from its proposed step. The tax enters as a penalty on that task's gradient. Under VCG, truthful "reporting" of gradients is the dominant strategy, so a task cannot game the allocation by inflating its loss. *Why new:* gradient-conflict methods have no notion of incentive compatibility. This matters once tasks are separate agents or users sharing a model (federated or multi-tenant CL) who might strategically misreport. *First test:* multi-tenant fine-tuning where one tenant up-weights its loss 5×, comparing VCG taxes with PCGrad and plain summation on everyone else's retention. Control: per-tenant loss normalization.

**E16. Staggered-term committees (Senate memory).** The US Senate keeps institutional memory despite elections because only a third of seats turn over at once. A committee of K models is retrained on a staggered schedule, so at any time most members carry older knowledge. Predictions come from a vote weighted by tenure and by recent accuracy. Knowledge survives complete turnover of every member. *Why new:* ensembles in CL are trained synchronously. Staggering is a structural institution that makes the committee's memory horizon longer than any member's, with a predicted horizon of K × term. *First test:* 3 small models on a drifting stream, staggered against synchronized retraining at equal compute, measuring retention of a task last seen 2 terms ago. Control: a synchronized ensemble, and a single model with replay at equal compute.

**E17. Double-entry bookkeeping for weights.** Every accepted update is recorded as a journal entry that debits the tasks it hurt and credits the tasks it helped, measured on probe baskets. It is stored as a low-rank delta tagged with its counterparties. The books must balance: an update whose debits to protected accounts exceed a limit is rejected. Any entry can be *reversed* by subtracting its delta. *Why new:* it gives a model an auditable ledger of transactions, which enables surgical reversal of the specific update that caused damage. Today the tools are checkpoint rollback or global unlearning. *First test:* LoRA steps on a 0.5B model with every 50-step delta stored, injecting one harmful batch at step 400. Identify and reverse the offending entry from the ledger, against rolling back to the checkpoint before it. Measure the good learning lost. Control: checkpoint rollback, and task arithmetic with per-task deltas.

**E18. Masterpiece exams for model succession.** Medieval guilds promoted a journeyman to master only after a *masterpiece* judged by current masters. Before a successor model replaces the incumbent, the incumbent writes an exam from its own competence frontier: hard cases it solves confidently that are rare in recent data. The successor must pass. Failed items become targeted training. *Why new:* regression suites are fixed and written by humans. Here the outgoing model authors the succession test from its own knowledge, so tacit skills nobody wrote down still get checked. *First test:* the incumbent picks its 200 highest-margin rare-class items, the successor is fine-tuned on a shifted stream, and we compare how much forgetting the masterpiece set catches against a random held-out set of the same size. Control: random held-out set, and a loss-ranked set.

**E19. Inheritance tax on generational distillation.** When knowledge passes from one model generation to the next by distillation, only a taxed fraction transfers automatically (the teacher's top-confidence subset). The rest must be re-earned on fresh data. Dynasties of stale knowledge therefore cannot persist without being re-validated. *Why new:* full-inheritance distillation passes errors and outdated beliefs on forever. A tax rate trades retention against freshness and should have an interior optimum under label drift. *First test:* 6 generations of distillation on a stream where 20% of labels flip per generation, sweeping the tax rate, measuring current accuracy and stable-fact retention. Control: full distillation plus fresh data at equal steps.

**E20. Quadratic funding for shared fleet memory.** In a fleet sharing a memory store, a lesson's slot priority is set by quadratic funding: (Σ√endorsement_i)². Many small endorsements from *diverse* agents count more than one agent's strong vote. The result is broadly useful lessons over ones a single agent overfits to. *Why new:* fleet-memory pooling has used votes and surprisingly-popular scoring (H1, killed). QF is a different aggregator with a known optimality property for public goods, and it rewards breadth of support explicitly. *First test:* 10 agents on 5 task families sharing a 20-slot memory, ranked by QF against summed endorsements, measuring held-out-family accuracy. Control: summed endorsement, and a count of distinct endorsers.

## Evolution and genetics

**E21. Muller's ratchet and recombination for edit streams.** In an asexual lineage, small harmful mutations pile up irreversibly: the least-loaded class is lost by drift and cannot come back. A single model under many small edits does the same. The proposed fix is sex. Two sibling lineages trained on the same stream in different orders and seeds are recombined layer-wise, picking per layer the parent with the lower probe-basket loss. Recombination should purge the accumulated load. *Why new:* model merging is usually a one-shot average. Treating it as periodic recombination predicts a ratchet speed and when recombination pays off. *First test:* two GPT-2 lineages under 500 small LoRA edits each, with layer-wise crossover every 100 edits, against either parent and plain averaging. Measure accumulated off-target damage. Control: weight averaging and TIES at the same intervals.

**E22. Gene duplication and divergence.** Before learning a new task, duplicate the units most relevant to it (a gene duplication). One copy is frozen and keeps the old function. The other is free to diverge for the new task (neofunctionalization). Redundant copies that nobody uses are pruned later (pseudogenization). *Why new:* it differs from adding fresh units (progressive nets) and from freezing (PackNet). Duplication starts the new skill *from the old function*, which predicts faster learning on related tasks and zero interference. *First test:* split CIFAR-100 MLP, duplicate the top-Fisher units against an equal number of fresh random units, measuring forward transfer and forgetting. Control: fresh units with equal parameter growth.

**E23. Plasmids: portable skill modules for drifting hosts.** Bacteria share skills through plasmids: small self-contained modules that work in many hosts. Train LoRA "plasmids" with *host-drift augmentation*: during training, randomly perturb or fine-tune the base along plausible future drift directions. A skill learned today then still works after the base model has continued to learn. *Why new:* adapters are tied to one frozen base. Adapters built to survive base continual learning would let skills persist outside the main weights and be reinserted, which is horizontal skill transfer. *First test:* a LoRA on base v0, then the base fine-tuned on 3 unrelated tasks, comparing the plasmid with a plain LoRA on the drifted base. Control: a plain LoRA trained with equal steps plus weight-noise regularization.

**E24. Effective population size of a replay buffer.** Population genetics says a rare variant is lost by drift at rate ~1/N_e, and N_e can be far below the census size N when offspring numbers vary. A replay buffer is a population, and reservoir sampling is Wright–Fisher. The predictions: rare-class *features* (not just labels) are lost at ~1/N_e, and a buffer with fixed family sizes (every stored item leaves exactly one descendant per refresh, Moran-style) roughly doubles N_e and halves the loss rate. *Why new:* buffer design has been heuristic. This gives a quantitative law, time-to-loss ∝ N_e, and a designed sampler that follows from it. *First test:* a class-imbalanced stream measuring time-to-loss of rare-class probes against reservoir size, then fixed-family sampling at equal size. Control: class-balanced reservoir.

**E25. Red Queen parasites for forgotten skills.** Keep a small co-evolving parasite generator whose fitness is finding inputs on which the current model has lost old-task competence. The host trains on parasite finds, and the parasite keeps moving to wherever the host is currently weakest. *Why new:* adversarial replay exists. The Red Queen framing adds a specific prediction: cycling of host weaknesses with a period set by parasite and host learning-rate ratio, and the claim that a fast parasite reduces *peak* forgetting more than a strong one. *First test:* split MNIST with a small conditional generator rewarded for old-task host errors, against generative replay at equal compute. Measure peak per-task drop. Control: generative replay without adversarial fitness.

**E26. Baldwinian assimilation of lessons into weights.** The Baldwin effect: behaviour first learned in each lifetime gets genetically assimilated over generations, so it becomes innate. Lessons an agent re-learns in context every episode are slowly assimilated into weights. The pressure toward assimilation comes from the *cost of in-context learning*: tokens and steps to rediscover. Only lessons with high rediscovery cost are assimilated. *Why new:* the ledger's Baldwin vs Lamarck (A2) asked which inheritance mode is better. This instead uses rediscovery cost as the selection pressure deciding *which* lessons move into weights. *First test:* a router task where an agent rediscovers lessons in context, measuring steps-to-rediscover per lesson, then assimilating the top-k by cost against top-k by frequency into LoRA. Control: assimilate by frequency, and assimilate everything.

**E27. Founder effects in fleet memory.** When a fleet splits (new deployment, new team), the daughter memory is a small sample of the parent's. Founder theory predicts loss of rare lessons and fixation of arbitrary ones, at rates set by founder size. The primitive is *founder-aware forking*: oversample rare-but-validated lessons at fork time so the daughter's expected diversity matches the parent's. *Why new:* it gives a quantitative account of how agent memory diverges under forking, with a fix grounded in conservation genetics (genetic rescue). *First test:* 30 forks of a 200-lesson memory at founder sizes 10–50, measuring rare-lesson loss against the founder prediction, then founder-aware sampling. Control: random fork, and top-utility fork.

**E28. Clonal expansion on recall.** The adaptive immune system keeps a few memory cells per antigen. On re-exposure they expand clonally and hypermutate toward the drifted antigen, then contract. On recall of an old skill, clone its units, hypermutate the clones with fast small-step training on the drifted inputs, keep the best, and prune back afterwards. *Why new:* CL treats recall as reading a fixed memory. Expand–mutate–contract treats recall as a short burst of local evolution, which suits tasks that recur in drifted form (seasonal domains). *First test:* rotated-MNIST tasks that recur with an added 15° rotation, comparing clonal expansion with fine-tuning the old units in place at equal compute, measuring recovery speed and side damage. Control: fine-tune in place.

**E29. Matriarch memory for rare catastrophes.** Elephant matriarchs remember droughts that happen once in 40 years, and herds led by old matriarchs survive better. Keep a frozen old model as an *elder*. The live model consults the elder only when its own uncertainty or surprise spikes on inputs out of the recent regime. Rare regimes the live model has forgotten are served from the elder, and the elder is replaced only when a new rare regime is learned. *Why new:* old snapshots are used for distillation or rollback. Uncertainty-gated deferral to an elder makes rare-regime knowledge zero-cost when unused and zero-forgetting. *First test:* a stream with a rare regime every 40 tasks, comparing the elder gate with replay at equal storage, measuring rare-regime accuracy on return and overhead. Control: replay with a stored rare-regime buffer of equal bytes.

**E30. Stemmatic reconstruction of lost skills.** Philologists rebuild a lost original text from many corrupted manuscript copies by inferring their family tree (stemma) and voting along it. Several descendants of one ancestor model, each fine-tuned in a different direction, have each lost the ancestral skill in different places. Infer their phylogeny from weight deltas and reconstruct the ancestral weights by tree-weighted parsimony per coordinate. *Why new:* merging methods (TIES, averaging) treat descendants as exchangeable. A phylogeny-aware merge corrects for shared ancestry: two siblings from one branch count once. That should recover a skill no single descendant still has. *First test:* a base trained on task A, 6 descendants in a 2-level tree fine-tuned on different tasks, recovering A by tree-weighted reconstruction against averaging and TIES. Control: TIES, and per-coordinate median.

## Culture and knowledge transmission

**E31. The Tasmania effect: a critical population for cumulative culture.** Henrich's model says skills are lost when a population is too small to offset imperfect copying: after isolation, Tasmania lost bone tools and fishing. In a fleet that learns by imitating its best member with transmission noise, the number of skills sustained should collapse below a critical fleet size N* = f(skill difficulty, copy fidelity). *Why new:* it gives a quantitative, falsifiable law for how fleet size and lesson-copy fidelity set the fleet's sustainable memory, with a phase transition rather than a smooth curve. *First test:* simulated agents (small LLM or synthetic skills with noisy copying), sweeping N and fidelity, checking for a sharp drop at the predicted N*. Control: independent learners with no imitation at the same N.

**E32. Metered oral tradition: self-correcting lesson memory.** Oral cultures keep epics stable for centuries through meter, rhyme and formulae: redundant constraints that make copying errors detectable and fixable by the next singer. Store agent lessons in a checkable meter (fixed slot structure, rhyme or checksum words, formulaic epithets). Every rewrite or consolidation cycle can then detect and repair drift against the constraint. *Why new:* iterated learning (ledger #17) studies drift. Here a transmission code is *designed* to make drift self-correcting, which also predicts which lesson contents survive. *First test:* a 30-generation paraphrase chain of 20 lessons through Qwen2.5-1.5B, metered against free form at equal length, measuring semantic fidelity at generation 30. Control: free form with a "preserve meaning exactly" instruction.

**E33. Ritual rehearsal: arbitrary anchors.** Rituals are repeated performances whose content can be arbitrary, yet they hold groups together. During later training, rehearse a fixed set of *arbitrary* input–output pairs (random inputs with the model's own frozen outputs at ritual creation) unrelated to any task. The claim: a meaningless but fixed function-space anchor slows representational drift and so protects real skills as a side effect. *Why new:* replay rehearses real data and anchors are usually task-related. Showing that *content-free* rehearsal protects knowledge would isolate drift-anchoring as a mechanism separate from rehearsal of content. *First test:* split CIFAR with 200 random-noise ritual inputs replaying their initial logits, against no replay and real-data replay of 200 items, measuring old-task accuracy. Control: real replay of equal size, and L2-to-init at matched strength.

**E34. Parables: compress lessons into fictional episodes.** Societies compress history into myths that drop the facts but keep the actionable moral. Consolidate agent lessons into short fictional *parables*: invented episodes that show the lesson in a different surface domain. Use those parables, not explicit rules, as the memory or fine-tune data. *Why new:* lesson memories are usually stored as rules or real trajectories. Parables are a third form that may transfer further, because the model must abstract the moral to apply it. *First test:* 20 router lessons stored as rules, parables, or real episodes at equal tokens, measuring held-out-domain accuracy, with the A3 temperature-matched and filler arms. Control: same-length filler, temperature-matched no-lesson arm, and explicit rule.

**E35. Language death and revival.** Hebrew was revived from scripture plus a small community of committed speakers. When a skill is fully forgotten, revive it from a *speaker community*: a tiny frozen model that still has the skill and acts as an interlocutor on the live model's own outputs (on-policy correction). The corpus alone is a weaker source. The primitive is the *revival potential*: the minimum speaker size plus corpus needed to restore a dead skill. *Why new:* recovery work measures relearning from data. Revival through on-policy interaction with a tiny speaker may need orders of magnitude less, and its scaling curve is unmeasured. *First test:* forget a task completely in a 0.5B model, then revive it with (a) corpus only or (b) on-policy feedback from a 50M speaker, at equal tokens. Measure the revival curve. Control: corpus-only revival, and off-policy distillation from the speaker.

**E36. Norm cascades and threshold diversity.** In Granovetter's model, people adopt a norm once a fraction θ_i of their neighbours has. Homogeneous thresholds produce sudden cascades, including of bad norms. In fleets that adopt lessons from peers, use *heterogeneous adoption thresholds* by design so bad lessons cannot cascade while good ones still spread. *Why new:* fleet-memory sharing usually uses one global rule. Threshold heterogeneity is a structural lever with a known phase diagram for cascade size. *First test:* 30 agents on a graph sharing a mix of good and bad lessons, homogeneous against diverse θ, measuring bad-lesson cascade rate and good-lesson spread time. Control: homogeneous θ tuned to the best single value.

**E37. Lindy protection.** By the Lindy effect, a non-perishable thing that has survived t years can expect roughly t more. Protect parameters, lessons or adapters in proportion to how long they have *survived* unchanged under ongoing training, not by gradient importance. *Why new:* it is a protection signal that needs no Fisher, labels or old data, only age. It predicts that survival time is a better retention prior than importance for long streams. *First test:* a 50-task stream with a protection penalty ∝ survival age against EWC at matched mean strength, measuring retention of early tasks. Control: EWC, and a random-protection mask.

**E38. Chesterton fence audits.** Before deleting a memory or pruning a parameter block, audit it the way Chesterton's fence suggests: find out what it guards. A *fence probe* searches for inputs where removing it changes behaviour (using the model's own generations as a probe pool), and the item is removed only if the probe finds nothing important within a budget. *Why new:* pruning and eviction use utility scores computed on *seen* data. A fence audit actively searches for unseen dependants, which matters for rare guarded cases. *First test:* evict 50% of a 100-lesson memory by utility, with and without fence audits, and measure rare-case regressions on held-out tasks. Control: utility eviction with equal compute spent on extra validation data.

## Specialization, trade and economic dynamics

**E39. Comparative-advantage forgetting in fleets.** Ricardo: even if one country is better at everything, both gain by specializing in their comparative advantage and trading. Each model in a fleet under a fixed capacity forgets on purpose. A fleet-level allocator assigns skills by comparative advantage (opportunity cost in retained capacity) under a coverage constraint (every skill has at least one holder). Queries are routed to the holder. *Why new:* fleet CL aims for every member to retain everything. Planned forgetting with trade gives a predicted gain over autarky that grows with skill heterogeneity. *First test:* 4 small MLPs on 8 tasks at fixed width, comparative-advantage allocation against every model learning all tasks, measuring fleet accuracy with routing. Control: random allocation with the same coverage.

**E40. Dutch disease sterilization.** A resource boom (a huge new data stream) crowds out other sectors through the exchange rate. A booming task does the same to other skills through shared representation. *Sterilize* the boom like a sovereign wealth fund: route a fraction of the booming task's gradient into a separate adapter (the fund), and convert it into shared weights only slowly, at a rate set by measured crowding-out. *Why new:* imbalanced-stream CL uses reweighting or replay. Sterilization isolates boom gains first and integrates them later at a controlled rate, which predicts less crowding-out than replay at equal compute. *First test:* a stream with one task at 10× data, comparing sterilization with replay and reweighting, measuring minority-task retention and booming-task accuracy. Control: loss reweighting.

**E41. Creative destruction with an IPO gate.** Schumpeter: incumbents are replaced by startups that do it better. Periodically, the module with the lowest profit (utility per parameter) is challenged by a freshly initialized startup module trained on that module's niche plus new data. The startup replaces the incumbent only if it beats the incumbent on a held-out market (an IPO), and the incumbent's best customers are migrated by distillation. *Why new:* continual backprop resets low-utility units unconditionally. The IPO gate and customer migration make reset a competitive selection step that cannot lose function. *First test:* the continual-backprop benchmark (slowly changing regression) with IPO-gated replacement against continual backprop, measuring plasticity over 1,000 tasks and old-function loss. Control: continual backprop and shrink-and-perturb.

**E42. Crop rotation and fallow layers.** Farmers rotate crops and leave fields fallow so the soil recovers. Rotate which third of layers is trainable per task, and let fallow layers regenerate by shrinking toward their initialization. Each layer's "crop history" (which task types it hosted) decides what it grows next, so the same type is never planted twice in a row. *Why new:* plasticity-loss remedies (shrink-and-perturb, resets) are applied to all layers at once. Rotation spreads recovery over time and space and keeps two thirds of the model producing. *First test:* 100-task permuted MNIST with 3-way rotation and fallow shrink, against global shrink-and-perturb at equal total shrink, measuring plasticity and retention. Control: global shrink-and-perturb.

**E43. Path-dependent standards as interface contracts.** QWERTY persists because everything downstream is built on it. In a modular continual learner, freeze an *interface standard* (a fixed intermediate code or codebook) that every module reads and writes. Module internals can then be replaced or relearned without breaking dependants, and the standard is versioned rarely, with adapters for migration. *Why new:* modular CL lets interfaces drift with training. A deliberately frozen, versioned standard is an institutional answer to cascading breakage, close to but distinct from composition contracts (ledger). *First test:* a 3-module pipeline with a frozen 64-code codebook interface, retraining module 1 on a new task and measuring downstream breakage against an end-to-end-trained interface. Control: end-to-end training with feature distillation on the interface.

**E44. Epigenetic silencing that decays across generations.** Epigenetic marks (methylation) switch genes off without deleting them, and they are partly inherited and fade over generations. Give each skill a learned binary gating mask over frozen weights (silencing instead of deleting). The masks are inherited by successor models at a decaying strength, so silenced skills come back if not re-silenced. *Why new:* supermasks select subnetworks per task. Transgenerational decay of silencing is a time-aware deletion primitive: a skill is suppressed now, its weights are kept, and it returns unless actively suppressed again. That is relevant to reversible unlearning. *First test:* silence one task in a split-CIFAR model with a mask, distill to a successor with mask inheritance at decay 0.5, and measure suppression and recovery over 3 generations. Control: permanent mask, and fine-tune-based unlearning.

**E45. Brain drain and knowledge migration.** Knowledge moves with people, and an exodus of experts empties a region. When fleet agents are reassigned between task domains, their memories go with them. Measure *knowledge emigration*: what the departing agent's domain loses and what the destination gains. Design an exit interview, a short distillation of the departing agent's domain-specific lessons into the shared domain memory before it leaves. *Why new:* fleet memory treats agents as interchangeable. Exit-interview distillation targets the specific loss when an agent moves on, a turnover event that current agent memory systems ignore. *First test:* 8 agents across 4 domains with random reassignment every 50 episodes, comparing domain accuracy after reassignment with and without exit-interview distillation into shared memory. Control: periodic sync of all memories at equal token cost.
