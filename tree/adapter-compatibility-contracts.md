# Adapter compatibility contracts: what a base update owes the adapters built on it

Parent: [PR #115](https://github.com/manojbajaj95/cl/pull/115) (`tree/breadth-outside-edit-theory.md`, "populations sharing weights"), its idea 11 "Adapter ABI for shared-base updates", and flood-triage P19 "stable ABI across base-model upgrades". **Node question:** when a shared base model is updated, every adapter or skill built on it can silently break. What contract must base updates preserve so downstream adapters keep working, and can that contract itself be the continual-learning object?

**Short answer.** The parent's contract is the wrong side of the interface. An adapter does two things: it *reads* activations at its insertion point, and it *writes* a low-rank change that the rest of the base then consumes. The parent ABI pins what adapters read. Our CPU probe shows that pinning reads makes client damage *worse* than plain base retention (worst client 9.8% vs 17.3%). The pinned layer forces the update into the downstream layers, and those downstream layers are what turn each adapter's write into its output. A contract on the downstream *response to each adapter's write subspace* recovers about 60% of the gap between base-only retention and full per-adapter distillation. It needs only the adapters' column spaces (no client data, no labels, no adapter inputs). Adding the read–write coupling through random adapters drawn from each client's (read span) × (write span) reaches 88% of the gap, matching distillation of the actual adapters, but here it costs 14 points of new-task accuracy at the same \(\lambda\). That points to the candidate primitive for this node: **a registry of adapter (read, write) subspaces whose bilinear response the base must preserve**. This registry is a memory that grows with deployment. Old base tasks can be registered in it too, as self-clients.

## Root cause: compatibility is a property of the downstream operator, not of the activation

Pool coverage check (PR #6 `reviews/idea-ledger.md`, `reviews/flood-triage.md`, `reviews/flood-top10.md`; `ideas/flood/programs-weights.md` P6/P17–P20; `ideas/flood/systems-db.md` SD24; PR #115 idea 11): the pool already has (a) P19/idea 11, fixed anchor activations as a calling convention, (b) P6, decompile–recompile skills across versions (triaged as Trans-LoRA / LoRA-X overlap), and (c) SD24, version-pinned skill slices (which is just pinning). Everything in the pool states compatibility as *equality of something the adapter sees*. Nothing states it as *equality of what happens to what the adapter emits*.

Take an adapter inserted at layer \(\ell\), with \(z_\ell \leftarrow z_\ell + B^\top A^\top h_{\ell-1}\), and the rest of the network \(g_\theta\) after the insertion point. The adapted output is \(g_\theta(z_\ell(h) + B^\top A^\top h)\). Writing \(w = B^\top A^\top h\), to first order this is \(g_\theta(z_\ell) + J_g(z_\ell)\,w\). A base update \(\theta \to \theta'\) breaks the adapter through three separate channels:

1. **Read drift.** \(h_{\ell-1}\) changes, so the adapter computes a different \(w\). The parent ABI controls this channel.
2. **Base drift.** \(g_\theta(z_\ell)\) changes. Ordinary base retention (distilling the base's own outputs) controls this channel.
3. **Consumer drift.** \(J_g\) restricted to \(\mathrm{span}(B)\) changes, so the same write means something different downstream. Nothing in the pool controls this channel.

Pinning channel 1 with a hard activation constraint pushes the update's degrees of freedom into \(g\), which inflates channel 3. That is why read-ABI loses to base-safe in the probe below. The adapter's effect also lives in a subspace that is tiny (rank \(r\)) and specific to each client. A base update that is excellent on average can therefore rotate \(J_g\) exactly along a client's \(B\). This is the population-level forgetting from PR #115: the base keeps its own behaviour while a client loses theirs.

**Why existing work does not close it.** PortLLM ([2410.10870](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10870)) carries patches forward without training and assumes the update is small. ReLoRA ([2606.02606](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02606)), Trans-PEFT ([2506.06844](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06844)), Heirloom ([2611.00650](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00650)), GRAFT ([2611.18638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18638)) and Trans-LoRA/LoRA-X re-adapt or map the *adapter* after the base has moved: the client pays, and needs data or a bridge. UpgradeBench ([2608.20918](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.20918)) measures portability decaying with continued-pretraining distance and treats it as a retain/port/refresh/retrain decision. Backward-compatible representation learning ([2506.05826](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05826), [2408.08793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08793), [2503.14824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14824), [2606.12488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12488)) and MUSCLE ([2407.09435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09435)) constrain the *base's own outputs or embeddings* for downstream consumers that only read them. They are channel-1/2 contracts for consumers that never write back. READ ([2609.31600](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31600)) and backbone-specific LoRA geometry ([2611.27201](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27201)) describe adapter geometry but do not constrain base updates with it. The closest framing is [2608.22462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22462) (functional compatibility as a determinant of persistent learning), which is about a single learner. **Overlap risk to check before any claim:** the first-order version of the write contract is a Jacobian-matching penalty along given subspaces. Jacobian/Sobolev distillation and EWC-style per-direction stiffness are known; the possibly-new part is that the subspaces come from a *population of deployed writers* and the contract is the object that persists.

### CPU probe ([`tree/probes/adapter_contract_probe.py`](probes/adapter_contract_probe.py), torch CPU, ~5 min, 5 seeds, mean ± SD)

A 3-layer MLP base (16→64→64→8) is trained on a base task. Eight private clients each fit a rank-4 LoRA on the middle layer for their own task, with the base frozen. The base then trains 600 steps on a new task under each contract (\(\lambda = 3\), the same for all arms), and every client's **unchanged** adapter is re-evaluated on the updated base. Writes are finite perturbations at the adapter's typical write scale. "Client probes" are 64 unlabeled client inputs. "Base inputs" are 64 base-task inputs.

| Before update | — | — | 52.6 ± 4.9 | 25.9 ± 14.1 | 77.1 ± 1.6 | 14.7 ± 12.1 |
| naive | nothing | — | 31.9 ± 4.4 | 14.0 ± 5.7 | 26.9 ± 8.3 | 81.9 ± 4.3 |
| base-safe | base outputs (channel 2) | base inputs | 36.7 ± 4.9 | 17.3 ± 11.2 | 68.1 ± 4.0 | 81.5 ± 5.0 |
| read-ABI (parent idea 11) | + h1, z2 activations at insertion (channel 1) | client probes | 33.5 ± 6.6 | **9.8 ± 7.0** | 68.9 ± 3.3 | 76.4 ± 6.3 |
| write-ctr | read-ABI + response to each \(B_c\) direction (channel 3) | client probes, span(B) | 45.5 ± 4.5 | 20.3 ± 9.1 | 74.5 ± 2.4 | 73.4 ± 6.9 |

**Readout.**
- **The parent ABI is worse than doing nothing extra.** read-ABI pins the insertion-point activations almost exactly (z2 drift 0.21 vs 10.6 for base-safe), yet clients get worse: mean 33.5 vs 36.7, worst 9.8 vs 17.3. Activation drift is anti-informative about adapter survival, because pinning reads moves the change into the consumer (channel 3).
- **Contracts on the consumer of writes work, without client data.** write-ctr closes 59% of the base-safe → composite gap (45.5 vs 36.7 → 51.5). write-base, which uses only span(B) and generic base inputs, is just as good (45.9, 62%). The write contracts also *raise* base old-task accuracy (74–75 vs 68), because a response contract regularizes the base in directions that matter.
- **Subspaces alone reach the composite level for clients.** span-rand never sees the trained adapter, only its read and write spans, and matches comp-base (49.8 vs 49.8, 88% of the gap). The new-task price is real: 66.9 vs 80.2 at the same \(\lambda\). Whether that is the cost of protecting a whole adapter class or a \(\lambda\) artifact is sub-question 1.
- **Client data is nearly unnecessary.** comp-base (base inputs) vs composite (client probes): 49.8 vs 51.5. The contract is carried by the adapter's geometry, not by the client's inputs.
- **Pooling costs about 3 points** (write-pool 42.5 vs 45.5), and the loss falls on the worst client, so contract compression (sub-question 5) is a real question.
- **Multi-scale writes did not matter here** (write-pm vs write-ctr: +0.8, within noise), so in this toy the consumer is close to linear at the adapters' write scale.

**Caveats.** This is a toy MLP with one \(\lambda\) and no sweep, so the new-task cost of the write contracts (about 7–8 points) may be partly a \(\lambda\) artifact. A Pareto sweep at equal new-task accuracy is the first thing to run before quoting any number. The adapters are weak (52.6% before the update, chance 12.5%). Only one base update is applied, not a sequence.

## Raw ideas (22)

Shared retention check for every idea, at equal base-update compute and the same new-task accuracy (Pareto, not one \(\lambda\)): (i) base old-task accuracy, (ii) unchanged-adapter accuracy for each client, mean **and worst**, (iii) the same numbers after a *second and third* base update, against base-safe, composite (an upper bound that needs adapters) and retune (an upper bound that needs client data). Each idea below lists only its specific extra check.

1. **Write-response contract (WRC).** *Mechanism:* register each adapter's write subspace \(\mathrm{span}(B_c)\). Base updates must keep the finite downstream response \(g(z + s\,b) - g(z)\) for \(b \in B_c\) at a few signed scales. *Why new:* every existing contract is on reads or base outputs; this is a contract on the consumer of writes (channel 3). *Cheapest test:* done above (write-ctr / write-pm / write-base). *Retention:* the shared check plus base old-task accuracy, which WRC *improved* (74.5 vs 68.1).
2. **Bilinear read–write registry.** *Mechanism:* register both \(\mathrm{span}(A_c)\) and \(\mathrm{span}(B_c)\). Preserve the base's output under random adapters \(A_c M\,N B_c\) drawn from the product span, on generic inputs, so the contract covers the read–write coupling that WRC misses. *Why new:* it constrains an adapter *class* rather than a fixed adapter. A client can disclose subspaces without its trained weights, and the contract also covers the client's future retraining inside the same subspaces. *Cheapest test:* done: span-rand matches comp-base on clients (49.8 vs 49.8) but loses 13 points on the new task. Next, a λ Pareto sweep. *Retention:* per-client accuracy after the client *retrains* its adapter inside the same subspaces post-update.
3. **Contract set as the old-task memory (self-clients).** *Mechanism:* the base's own old tasks get small probe adapters fitted to them, and those adapters' (read, write) subspaces are registered like any client's. Retention becomes contract preservation; there is no replay or logit distillation. *Why new:* it unifies base forgetting and client compatibility under one object. Forgetting is "breaking an adapter you wrote for yourself". *Cheapest test:* replace the base-safe KL term with self-client WRC terms of equal byte size and compare base old-task accuracy. *Retention:* base old-task accuracy vs equal-byte replay (the ledger's L1 lesson: equal-byte deltas beat clever stores).
4. **Socket subspace.** *Mechanism:* reserve a fixed \(k\)-dim write subspace \(S\) at each insertion layer. Base updates are projected so that \(g\) restricted to \(S\) stays fixed to first order, and adapters are trained to write only into \(S\). Compatibility then holds by construction for every future adapter. *Why new:* P19 fixes *anchor activations* (reads); a socket fixes the *consumer's response* on a reserved write space. *Cheapest test:* constrain the adapters' \(B\) to a shared random \(S\); measure client accuracy before the update and survival after it. *Retention:* client accuracy loss from the restriction to \(S\) before any update (the price of the socket).
5. **Vendor-shipped write transport.** *Mechanism:* after an unconstrained update, the base vendor solves for each registered write subspace a small \(r \times r\) transport \(T_c\) with \(J'_g B_c^\top T_c \approx J_g B_c^\top\), using only generic inputs, and ships \(T_c\). The client applies \(B_c \leftarrow T_c^\top B_c\). *Why new:* PortLLM and GRAFT map adapters and need the adapter or client data; here the contract registry lets the *base* emit the fix. *Cheapest test:* least-squares \(T_c\) on base inputs after the naive arm. *Retention:* client accuracy with \(T_c\) vs retune; base untouched.
6. **Contract certificates.** *Mechanism:* each base update emits a bound \(\sup_{b\in B_c}\lVert g'(z+b)-g(z+b)\rVert\), computed from spectral norms of the downstream weights restricted to the client subspace. Clients accept or pin the old version. *Why new:* it links to `tree/set-valued-edit-certs.md`, with the certified set being the adapter's write span rather than an edit ball. *Cheapest test:* correlation between the bound and the actual client accuracy drop across arms and seeds. *Retention:* false-accept rate (bound says safe, client drops by more than 5 points).
7. **Future-trainability contract.** *Mechanism:* preserve the adapter-parameter NTK \(\nabla_{A,B} f \cdot \nabla_{A,B} f^\top\) on client probes, so clients who keep fine-tuning learn the same way after the upgrade. *Why new:* every compatibility notion so far is static; this one is about the *dynamics* of downstream learners (the PR #115 growth finding that function preservation does not preserve dynamics, applied to adapters). *Cheapest test:* continue adapter training for 50 steps after the update; compare the learning curve with continued training on the old base. *Retention:* client accuracy at equal post-update client steps.
8. **Contract shadow prices.** *Mechanism:* solve the base update as a constrained problem with one constraint per client. The dual variables say which client's contract is blocking new learning. Expensive contracts are retired, renegotiated or merged into the base. *Why new:* it turns compatibility into an explicit economy over the population rather than one weighted penalty. *Cheapest test:* augmented-Lagrangian update in the probe; check that the duals rank clients by naive-arm damage. *Retention:* worst client under a fixed new-task target.
9. **Contract sketching.** *Mechanism:* compress all clients' write subspaces into a per-layer frequent-directions sketch whose size is independent of client count. *Why new:* in the probe, pooled SVD (write-pool, k = 4) lost about 3 points; the question is the compression law (accuracy vs sketch size vs client count). *Cheapest test:* sweep k ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16, 32} at NC ∈ {8, 32}. *Retention:* the *worst* client, since sketching protects common directions and abandons rare clients.
10. **Worst-client (DRO) base update.** *Mechanism:* minimize the maximum contract violation over clients instead of the mean. *Why new:* it defines retention as a population quantile, which no CL benchmark reports. *Cheapest test:* replace the mean with a soft-max over clients in write-ctr. *Retention:* worst client (currently 20.3%) vs mean.
11. **Gauge-fixed upgrades.** *Mechanism:* after an unconstrained update, choose the hidden-unit reparametrization of the new base (orthogonal or permutation at the insertion layers) that minimizes contract violation. The function is unchanged, so this costs nothing in base or new-task accuracy. *Why new:* re-basin methods align models for merging; here the gauge is chosen for downstream writers, post hoc. *Cheapest test:* orthogonal Procrustes on the insertion layer after the naive arm, measuring how much of the break is pure basis change. *Retention:* identical base accuracy by construction; client survival is the whole readout.
12. **Null-space growth for writers.** *Mechanism:* the new task updates only directions orthogonal to all registered read subspaces at each layer, and downstream changes orthogonal to all write subspaces (GPM, but with deployed adapter factors as the protected bases). *Why new:* the protected subspaces come from other learners' parameters, not the base's activations. *Cheapest test:* project the probe's base gradient. *Retention:* new-task accuracy cost as the number of clients grows (capacity exhaustion).
13. **Self-healing adapters from a broadcast residual.** *Mechanism:* the base broadcasts its response change on a canonical set of write directions. Each client applies a closed-form correction to its own \(B\) locally, with no data and no vendor access to the adapter. *Why new:* the inverse of idea 5, where privacy stays with the client and the contract is a public signal. *Cheapest test:* least squares of the client's \(B\) against the broadcast responses. *Retention:* client accuracy vs idea 5 and retune.
14. **Requires-clauses.** *Mechanism:* each adapter ships a small declarative spec (subspace, probe set, tolerance), and the base update solver is a dependency resolver that either satisfies every clause or reports which clients break. *Why new:* it makes compatibility a checked artifact, like package constraints, for neural modules. It is mostly systems; it is new only if the clauses are learned (idea 21). *Cheapest test:* report precision/recall of "will break" predictions from clause violations. *Retention:* missed breaks.
15. **Contract consolidation.** *Mechanism:* periodically distill popular adapters into the base and replace their contracts with smaller residual contracts. The contract set shrinks as the base absorbs skills, and the base's capability growth is measured as contract shrinkage. *Why new:* it gives a population CL loop in which knowledge flows client → base, with the contract as bookkeeping. *Cheapest test:* absorb two of the eight clients and check that the remaining six survive. *Retention:* the absorbed clients served by the base alone vs by their adapters.
16. **Composition-closed contracts.** *Mechanism:* clients merge or stack adapters, so the contract must preserve pairwise interaction terms \(g(z+w_1+w_2)-g(z+w_1)-g(z+w_2)+g(z)\), not just single writes. *Why new:* merging work (READ, `tree/merger-certificates.md`) assumes a fixed base; this asks which base updates keep merges valid. *Cheapest test:* merged-pair accuracy after the update under WRC vs a pairwise-WRC. *Retention:* single-adapter accuracy.
17. **Contracts learned from failure reports.** *Mechanism:* start with no contracts. When a client reports a break, fit the minimal contract term (the subspace and scale that explains the break) and add it for all future updates. The contract is learned continually from incidents. *Why new:* the contract itself becomes the continual learner, growing from sparse feedback. *Cheapest test:* three sequential base updates where breaks are reported only after the first. *Retention:* breaks on the 2nd and 3rd updates for reported vs unreported clients.
18. **Contract drift as a label-free forgetting detector.** *Mechanism:* the write-response drift on registered subspaces is cheap to compute without labels and should predict client accuracy loss better than activation drift. The probe already shows that activation drift is anti-informative (read-ABI has the lowest z2 drift and among the worst client accuracies). *Why new:* it inverts the usual drift monitor, which watches activations. *Cheapest test:* rank correlation of each drift metric with client accuracy loss across arms × seeds × clients. *Retention:* not an intervention; report false negatives.
19. **Two-timescale co-evolution.** *Mechanism:* the base and adapters update alternately. The base keeps contracts within ε, and adapters spend a small budget re-fitting inside their registered subspaces. Find the ε that maximizes total population accuracy per unit compute. *Why new:* it treats freeze-the-base vs retune-every-adapter as two ends of one continuum with an optimal interior point. *Cheapest test:* sweep ε × client budget in the probe. *Retention:* population accuracy over five base updates.
20. **Canonical adapter dictionary.** *Mechanism:* the base defines a fixed dictionary of rank-1 (read, write) atoms per layer, and adapters are sparse coefficient vectors over the atoms. Base updates preserve every atom's bilinear response, so every adapter is portable by construction. *Why new:* P19 anchors only reads; here whole read–write atoms form the ABI, which is idea 2 with a shared rather than per-client span. *Cheapest test:* restrict probe adapters to 64 random atoms; measure the expressivity loss and the survival gain. *Retention:* client accuracy before the update (the expressivity price).
21. **Learned contract tolerance.** *Mechanism:* fit a per-client map from contract violation to accuracy loss, and give each client the loosest tolerance that keeps its loss below δ. Robust clients release capacity to the new task. *Why new:* a uniform λ wastes capacity on robust adapters; this prices fragility per client. *Cheapest test:* fit violation-to-loss on naive and base-safe arms; rerun with per-client λ. *Retention:* new-task accuracy at equal worst-client accuracy.
22. **Second-order consumer contract.** *Mechanism:* preserve the curvature \(\partial^2 g\) along write directions (the finite responses at ±1 and ±2 scales already sample it), because nonlinear consumers can match first-order responses and diverge at large writes. *Why new:* it separates linear (Jacobian) compatibility from nonlinear compatibility, which predicts when cheap first-order contracts fail. *Cheapest test:* the write-ctr vs write-pm gap (+0.8 points here, within noise) as a function of the adapters' write scale. *Retention:* clients with the largest write norms.

## Deeper sub-questions, ranked by chance of a new primitive

1. **Is there a data-free, weight-free contract that matches per-adapter distillation?** Ideas 2, 20. The probe puts WRC at about 60% of the base-safe → composite gap without client data, and span-rand (subspaces only) at 88%, but at a 13-point new-task cost. The deciding experiment is a Pareto sweep: at *equal* new-task accuracy, does span-rand still beat WRC and match comp-base? If subspace-only contracts close most of the gap at equal plasticity, the primitive is "an adapter is registered by its subspaces, not its weights", and it covers future retrains as well. This is the highest chance because it is cheap to test, has a clear failure mode, and is not covered by PortLLM, ReLoRA or GRAFT.
2. **Can the contract registry replace replay as the base's own retention mechanism?** Ideas 3, 15. If self-client contracts match equal-byte replay or KL distillation on old tasks, base forgetting and client compatibility become one object that grows by registration and shrinks by consolidation. This would make a population CL primitive. It must pass the equal-byte test that killed L1.
3. **How much of an upgrade break is pure gauge?** Idea 11. If a function-preserving reparametrization after the update recovers most client accuracy, compatibility is mostly a coordinate problem, solved at zero cost to the base. That would be a clean, surprising result, or an equally clean negative one.
4. **Does preserving response preserve downstream learnability?** Ideas 7, 19. Static contracts may keep today's adapters working while changing how clients keep learning. A trainability contract would be a dynamics-level ABI, which is new if it holds.
5. **What is the contract compression law?** Ideas 9, 10. How many dimensions per layer are needed to protect N clients at worst-client tolerance δ? A law in N, rank and layer width would turn "can a shared base serve unbounded clients" into a capacity statement.
